**What Lawn Care Software Should Actually Cost (And Why Some Companies Won't Tell You)**

If you've started shopping for lawn care business software, you've probably noticed something strange: half the pricing pages don't have any prices on them.

You fill out a form. You "request a demo." Somebody calls you a few days later, walks you through a screen-share, and *then* tells you what it costs — usually after asking a bunch of questions about how big your operation is first.

That's not an accident. It's a sales tactic. If a company can get you on a call before you see the number, they can tailor the pitch, build rapport, and make it harder to just say "no thanks" and go compare somewhere else. For some businesses that's a fine tradeoff — enterprise software often works this way because the deals are genuinely complex. But for a solo operator or a 5-person crew trying to get off a paper invoice book? You shouldn't need a sales call to find out if something fits your budget.

**Here's what to actually expect to pay:**

Most lawn-care-specific software runs somewhere between $30–$150/month for a small operation, depending on how many crew members and customers you have. Some charge per user/seat on top of the base price — which sounds fine until you're paying for every part-time mower operator who touches a job, even ones who never log into the software at all.

A few tools genuinely have a free tier. Read the fine print, though — "free" sometimes means ad-supported, or capped so tightly it's really just an extended demo, or missing basic things like GPS tracking or bulk messaging until you upgrade.

**What we think "free" should mean:**

At Terra Verde, free means: no credit card to sign up, no countdown clock, and real features — scheduling, routing, invoicing — not a crippled trial dressed up as a free plan. You'll hit limits eventually if your business grows (that's how any free tier works), but you'll never be locked out of trying it because a 14-day clock ran out, and you'll never need a sales call to find out what the next tier costs.

If you're evaluating lawn care software right now, the quickest litmus test is this: can you see real pricing on the website without giving up your email and phone number first? If not, that's worth asking why.

**Why We Don't Charge You for Every Crew Member Who Touches a Mower**

Most lawn care software prices itself by the seat — meaning every person on your team who needs access costs you a monthly fee, on top of your base subscription.

That model makes sense for office software, where every user is sitting at a desk logging in every day. It makes a lot less sense for a lawn crew, where half your team might be seasonal, part-time, or someone who shows up, mows the route on their phone or a paper sheet, and never actually needs to "log into" anything.

**How Terra Verde handles this differently:**

You can add crew members to Terra Verde without giving them a login at all. They show up on the schedule, they're assigned to routes, the office can see their day — but if they're a scheduling-only crew member, you're not paying a per-seat fee for someone who just needs to be *on the schedule*, not *in the software*.

That matters more than it sounds like at first. A 3-person crew today might be a 3-person crew plus two seasonal helpers next spring. With a per-seat pricing model, that growth costs you real money every month, whether or not those extra people ever touch the app. With Terra Verde, you can staff up for the season without your software bill scaling right alongside it.

If you're comparing tools and one of your questions is "what happens to my bill when I hire someone," ask specifically whether that new hire needs a paid seat just to show up on a route — the answer varies more than you'd think between tools that otherwise look similar.

## 4. Blog Post 3 — "Route Optimization vs. a Route Optimizer: What's Actually Different"

*Target keyword: "lawn crew route planning software." Translates the technical differentiator (2-opt + k-means) into plain outcomes — no algorithm names in the customer-facing copy per the plan's messaging guidance.*

---

**Route Optimization vs. a Route Optimizer: What's Actually Different**

Almost every piece of lawn care software these days advertises "route optimization." In practice, a lot of what gets called route optimization is really just route *ordering* — the software looks at your stops for the day and puts them in a reasonable-looking sequence on a map. That's better than nothing, but it's not the same thing as an actually efficient route.

Here's a simple way to tell the difference: ask what happens when you add a stop in the middle of an already-planned route, or when you're trying to split 40 stops across 3 crews fairly. A basic route orderer just tacks the new stop onto wherever it fits in the existing sequence. A real optimizer re-evaluates the whole route and looks for ways to shorten total drive time — sometimes even swapping the order of stops that are already planned if it saves meaningful distance.

**What this looks like day to day:**

Terra Verde's routing doesn't just sort your stops — it actively looks for shorter paths through them, and when you're running multiple crews, it groups nearby jobs together so each crew's day makes geographic sense instead of criss-crossing the same neighborhood twice. In practice, that means fewer miles driven, more stops fit into a workday, and less gas and windshield time eating into your margin on every job.

You don't need to understand the math behind it — you just need to notice that your Tuesday route doesn't have your crew driving past a customer's house twice because the software slotted a new stop in the wrong spot. If a route "optimizer" you're evaluating can't explain how it handles adding a stop mid-route or balancing work across multiple crews, it's worth asking — because that's usually where the real difference between "route ordering" and actual route optimization shows up.
